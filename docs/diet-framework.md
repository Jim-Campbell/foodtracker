# Jim Campbell — Diet Framework

*Calibrated for cardiovascular risk (Lp(a) 563, ApoB 89), protein optimization, and cognitive health. Supersedes standard MIND diet guidelines where evidence supports.*

*Version 5 — July 22, 2026. A working document, not a static reference: expanded from a contrast list into a fuller map so scoring tools do not return "neutral" on unlisted foods, and now revised against two weeks of actual logged intake.*

-----

## Default Rule (read this first)

If a food is **not listed** in this framework, do not score it neutral. Apply this decision cascade instead:

1. **Is it a minimally processed whole plant food?** → Hard Yes.
2. **Is it a lean, unprocessed animal protein?** → Hard Yes (fish, poultry, eggs, shellfish) or Soft Yes (red meat, pork).
3. **Is it high in saturated fat (>~4g per serving) without a fermented/aged food-matrix rationale?** → Soft No.
4. **Is it ultra-processed, deep-fried, refined-flour-based, or sugar-sweetened?** → Hard No.
5. **Otherwise** → Soft Yes, flag for review, and add it to this document.

**Governing principle:** saturated fat and ultra-processing are the two axes that move Jim's cardiovascular risk. Everything else is secondary.

## Hard Yes — Eat freely

**Vegetables & plants**
- Leafy greens
- Cruciferous vegetables (broccoli, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts, cabbage, kale)
- Vegetables generally
- Mushrooms
- Garlic, onions, herbs, spices
- Fermented vegetables (sauerkraut, kimchi) — *watch sodium*

**Fruit**
- Berries (priority — polyphenols, cognitive evidence)
- **Avocado** — monounsaturated fat, potassium, fiber; strong lipid-profile evidence *(added v2)*
- Olives *(added v2)*
- Whole fruit generally *(added v2)*

**Protein**
- Eggs
- Lean chicken and turkey
- Fatty fish (salmon, sardines, mackerel, herring, anchovies) — priority for omega-3 index
- Lean fish (cod, halibut, tilapia, sole)
- **Shellfish** (shrimp, scallops, mussels, oysters, crab) — very lean, high zinc/B12/selenium; dietary cholesterol concern is obsolete *(added v2)*
- Legumes (beans, lentils, chickpeas)
- **Minimally processed soy** (tofu, tempeh, edamame) — complete protein, low saturated fat *(added v2)*

**Fats**
- Olive oil (default cooking and finishing fat)
- **Avocado oil** — high smoke point, monounsaturated *(added v2)*
- Nuts
- **Seeds** (chia, flax, hemp, pumpkin, sunflower) *(added v2)*
- **Natural nut and seed butters** — ingredient list should be nuts + salt only *(added v2)*

**Grains**
- Whole grains (oats, quinoa, farro, brown rice, barley, buckwheat)
- Sprouted whole-grain bread (Ezekiel type) *(added v2)*

**Beverages**
- Water
- **Coffee and tea, unsweetened** — green tea and coffee both carry favorable cardiovascular and cognitive observational data *(added v2)*

## Hard No — Avoid

- Alcohol — **with a standing allowance: one drink per week, bankable.** See note below. Unbanked or routine drinking remains Hard No *(revised v5)*
- Processed meat (bacon, deli meats, sausage, hot dogs)
- Butter
- Fried and fast food
- Ultra-processed foods
- Refined flour / white bread
- **Sugar-sweetened beverages** (soda, energy drinks, sweetened coffee drinks) *(added v2)*
- **Partially hydrogenated oils / artificial trans fats** *(added v2)*

## Soft Yes — Enjoy in moderation (daily is fine)

- Hard aged cheese (Parmesan, Gouda, aged cheddar, Gruyère) — good K2, protein, leucine
- Fermented dairy: cottage cheese, Greek yogurt, kefir — full-fat versions are **not** a cardiovascular liability; the reason to default to nonfat/low-fat is protein density within the calorie budget, not saturated fat *(clarified v3 — see note below)*
- Lean unprocessed red meat — grass-fed beef, bison, venison (3-4x/week reasonable)
- Lean unprocessed pork (tenderloin, loin) — comparable to chicken breast in fat content, high B vitamins
- Dark chocolate (70%+)
- Whole grain / whole wheat pasta — lower GI than white, more fiber, counts as whole grain
- Legume-based pasta (chickpea, lentil, edamame) — higher protein and fiber than wheat pasta *(added v2)*
- **Whey or casein protein powder** — legitimate bridge to protein targets; minimal-ingredient isolates preferred; not a foundation *(added v2)*
- **Plant protein powders — including Orgain Organic Protein + 50 Superfoods** (current daily use, taken with creatine) — calorie-efficient and cardiovascularly clean, but lower in leucine than whey; see note below on pairing *(added v4)*
- **Starchy vegetables** (sweet potato, potato, winter squash, corn) — whole-food quality is fine, but glycemic load matters given HbA1c 5.7%; pair with protein and fat *(added v2)*
- **Dried fruit** — nutritionally intact but sugar-dense and easy to overeat; small portions *(added v2)*
- **Neutral seed oils** (canola, high-oleic sunflower) — acceptable when a neutral-flavor or high-heat oil is needed; olive oil remains the default *(added v2)*
- **Protein bars** (e.g. Pure Protein, think!) — count these as **dessert that happens to contain protein**, not as a protein source; see the protein efficiency note below *(added v2, reclassified v5)*
- **Non-alcoholic beer — PROTECTED. Never flag, never list as a cut target.** ~60-140 cal depending on brand, ~58 cal/day averaged. Serves as an alcohol substitute supporting sobriety, which directly supports the mood-stability constraint that makes alcohol a Hard No in the first place. Its behavioral value exceeds its caloric cost by a wide margin. Any analysis that proposes trimming it is optimizing the wrong variable *(added v5)*

## Soft No — Occasional, not regular

- Sweets and pastries
- Ice cream
- Soft / processed cheese (American, Velveeta, cheese spreads)
- Fatty cuts of grain-fed beef (ribeye, 80/20 ground beef)
- Fatty pork cuts (shoulder, belly, ribs)
- White flour products
- **Coconut oil, coconut cream, full-fat coconut milk** — ~85-90% saturated fat with no fermentation or food-matrix offset; the health-halo marketing is not evidence-based *(added v2)*
- **Palm oil** — high saturated fat, near-ubiquitous in packaged foods *(added v2)*
- **Fruit juice** — fiber removed, sugar concentrated; whole fruit instead *(added v2)*
- **Plant-based meat analogs** (Beyond, Impossible) — ultra-processed, high sodium, often coconut/palm fat; the plant framing does not exempt them *(added v2)*
- **Non-fermented full-fat dairy** (whole milk, cream, half-and-half) — the fermentation and food-matrix argument that rescues aged cheese and yogurt does not extend to these *(added v2, narrowed v3)*
- **High-sodium packaged foods** — canned soups, frozen entrées, most restaurant sauces *(added v2)*

## Notes

**Current targets** *(revised v5, derived rather than assumed):*

| | Target | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Calories | **2,050-2,100/day** | Maintenance 2,300-2,400 minus the 250-375 cal/day deficit required for the 0.5-0.75 lb/week rate goal |
| Protein | **170g/day** (160-180 range) | Muscle preservation at 67 with low testosterone; ~33% of calories at the above target |
| Saturated fat | **≤14g/day** | ~6% of calories, the high-risk threshold given Lp(a) 563 |

The prior 1,800-1,900 target was inconsistent with the stated rate goal — it implies 0.8-1.2 lb/week, faster than intended, and forces protein to 37% of calories, which is very hard to hit with real food. The corrected target is both more achievable and more aligned with the actual objective. **Two weeks of logging (July 2026) showed zero days under 1,900 and an average of 2,470 — effectively no deficit.** The gap to close is roughly 370 cal/day, not 620.

**Category is not calorie budget.** *(v2, still the most operationally useful line in this document.)* "Hard Yes" describes nutritional quality, not permission for unlimited quantity. Avocado, nuts, seeds, nut butters, and olive oil are all Hard Yes and all calorie-dense — a whole avocado is ~320 calories, two tablespoons of almond butter ~190. Measured over two weeks, nuts and nut butter alone averaged 185 cal/day and delivered ~3g of protein per day. A food can be a Hard Yes and still be the wrong choice on a given day.

*Note for the scoring app (revised v5):* rather than splitting quality and calorie-fit into two visible scores, the decided approach is a single quality score plus **automatic attribution on outlier days** — when a day deviates from the trailing baseline, surface the top contributing items and the specific reason. The score is an anomaly detector, not a grade.

**Avocado specifically:** Established — avocado consumption modestly lowers LDL and ApoB in controlled feeding trials, largely via monounsaturated fat displacing saturated fat. Inference — this is directionally useful for Jim's ApoB 89 but the effect size is small relative to statin/ezetimibe. Opinion — its real value is as a satiety and texture tool that makes lean, high-protein eating sustainable.

**Protein priority:** Target 170g/day (160-180 range) given age, low testosterone, and anabolic resistance. This framework is compatible with high protein intake — eggs, fish, shellfish, lean meat, Greek yogurt, soy, and legumes are all high-quality protein sources.

**Protein efficiency — calories per gram of protein.** *(new in v5. This turned out to be the single most clarifying metric in the July 2026 log analysis.)*

| Source | Cal per gram protein |
|---|---|
| Whey isolate | ~4.4 |
| Shrimp | ~4.5 |
| Chicken breast | ~4.5 |
| Nonfat Greek yogurt | ~5.5 |
| Orgain plant powder | ~7.8 |
| think! protein bar | ~11.2 |

At a fixed calorie ceiling, this number determines whether a protein target is reachable at all. **The practical consequence: protein bars are desserts with protein attached, not protein sources.** A 230-calorie bar delivering 20g is a perfectly good dessert and a poor way to buy protein. Counting bars in the protein column lets them displace sources that cost half as much. Log them against the treat budget instead.

Measured over two weeks in July 2026, 24% of total protein (549g of 2,328g) came from powders, bars, and shakes — high for items the framework classifies as bridges. Whole-food protein is more calorie-efficient, which is the constrained resource.

**Alcohol — allowance, not abstinence.** *(new in v5.)* Jim has given up routine drinking. The operating rule is **one drink per week, banked and accrued**, spent deliberately at events that warrant it — e.g. two glasses of wine at a special occasion representing two weeks of saved allowance. This is a quota, not a lapse, and should not be scored as a framework violation. The distinction matters for tracking: a planned draw against an allowance and an unplanned drink are different events, and conflating them makes the log less honest rather than more disciplined. Non-alcoholic beer serves as the everyday substitute and is protected (see Soft Yes).

**Saturated fat target.** *(new in v5.)* Established — for high cardiovascular risk, the guideline threshold is under ~6% of calories, which is roughly **14g/day** at a 2,050 cal target. Measured — July 2026 logging averaged **27g/day**, nearly double. Inference — this is the single metric in the framework that bears directly on Lp(a) 563 and ApoB 89 rather than on body composition, and it is the one furthest from target. Primary drivers in the log were fatty red meat cuts, cheese, nuts, and ice cream. Opinion — of everything in this document, closing this gap has the highest expected value per unit of effort, and it is currently untracked.

**Omega-3 gap:** Omega-3 index was 5.3% (Apr 2024), below the cardiovascular protective range of 8-11%. Fatty fish 2-3x/week plus fish oil supplementation (2-4g EPA+DHA daily) is the target. **Recheck overdue — reference figure is now over two years old; slated for August 2026 labs.**

**Red meat distinction:** The Hard No applies to *processed* red meat only. Unprocessed lean red meat (grass-fed beef, bison, venison) has weak evidence for cardiovascular harm at moderate consumption and provides high-quality protein, leucine, creatine, zinc, B12, and heme iron. Grass-fed preferred for better omega-6:omega-3 ratio.

**Orgain Organic Protein + 50 Superfoods (daily, with creatine):** *(new in v4.)*

*Established (label data, July 2026):* ~160 cal and 21g protein per 2-scoop serving. Protein blend is pea, brown rice, chia, and mung bean. Also contains a creamer base (acacia, high-oleic sunflower oil, rice dextrin, sunflower lecithin), erythritol, a 50-item superfoods blend, probiotics, and ~8g prebiotic fiber. 1g sugar, no added sugar. Formulations vary by SKU and change over time — verify against the actual tub.

*Calorie efficiency is adequate.* Protein supplies ~53% of the serving's calories, above the ~33% daily average the 2,050 cal / 170g targets require. It is not a drag on the ratio. It is simply less efficient than whey isolate (~91%) or nonfat Greek yogurt (~72%) — see the protein efficiency table above.

*Decision (v5): keep it, add whey.* The superfoods blend, fiber, and plant base are the reasons for taking it and are worth keeping. Add 15-20g whey isolate to the same shake — ~80 calories for ~18g protein at the best available ratio, which brings the combined bolus over the leucine threshold that the Orgain alone does not clear. Stack rather than swap.

*The real limitation is leucine.* Pea and rice proteins run roughly 6.5-8% leucine, so 21g delivers ~1.6-1.7g. Whey at 25g delivers ~2.75g. **Inference:** anabolic resistance with age raises the leucine threshold for maximally stimulating muscle protein synthesis to roughly 2.5-3g per bolus. A single serving taken alone likely falls short. Given that muscle preservation under low testosterone at 67 is a top-tier priority, this matters.

*Practical fix — stack, do not drop.* Take it within a meal that already contains protein so the combined bolus clears threshold, or add a scoop of whey isolate to the same shake, or move the protein base to whey and keep the greens/fiber component separate. The choice depends on whether the product is being used for protein or for the fiber and micronutrient blend.

*Heavy metals flag.* **Established:** rice protein concentrate accumulates inorganic arsenic; pea and cocoa-based plant powders concentrate cadmium and lead. **Uncertain:** the magnitude reported in Clean Label Project testing is contested and the methodology has credible critics. **Opinion:** for sustained daily use over years, rotate protein sources rather than running a single rice-containing powder indefinitely. Not an urgent concern; a reason not to autopilot.

*Creatine (taken alongside):* Keep it. Among the best-evidenced supplements for muscle preservation, with emerging but smaller-trial cognitive data relevant to APOE e4 status. Effect size is larger in people with lower dietary creatine intake, so pairing it with plant-protein days is sensible. Creatine is a supplement rather than a food and sits outside the Hard/Soft categorization.

**Full-fat fermented dairy:** *(new in v3.)* Established — meta-analyses of fermented dairy show neutral-to-favorable cardiovascular associations, and stratification by fat content generally does not show full-fat performing worse; yogurt is the most-studied case. The fermentation and food-matrix rationale that applies to aged cheese applies to yogurt and kefir as well. Inference — full-fat yogurt is therefore not a cardiovascular problem for Jim, and the earlier v2 Soft No entry was an overreach. The reason to default to nonfat Greek yogurt is arithmetic: ¾ cup nonfat is ~100 cal / ~18g protein, full-fat 5% is ~155 cal / ~15g protein. More protein for fewer calories matters at 170g inside 2,050 cal — the same logic that favors chicken breast over thigh. Opinion — use full-fat when it is doing culinary work (sauce base, sour cream replacement, or where texture determines whether the meal actually gets eaten); default to nonfat for the daily protein-vehicle role.

**Cheese rationale:** Hard aged cheeses are neutral to modestly beneficial for cardiovascular outcomes despite saturated fat content — the food matrix effect (calcium binding, K2 content, fermentation) modifies metabolic impact. 1-2 oz daily of aged cheese is not a cardiovascular liability. Soft/processed cheese lacks these benefits and is high sodium. **This rationale is specific to aged, fermented cheese and does not generalize to other full-fat dairy.**

**Seed oil position:** *(new in v2.)* Established — no strong human outcome evidence that canola or sunflower oil causes cardiovascular harm; replacing saturated fat with them improves lipid markers. Opinion — the anti-seed-oil movement is currently outrunning its evidence. Practical stance: olive oil is the default because the evidence base for it is deeper, not because seed oils are dangerous.

**Shellfish and dietary cholesterol:** *(new in v2.)* Established — dietary cholesterol has a weak effect on serum cholesterol in most people; the 2015 US dietary guidelines dropped the limit. Shrimp and scallops are among the leanest high-protein foods available and are well suited to the calorie-constrained, protein-dense pattern Jim is running.

**MIND diet context:** This framework incorporates MIND diet principles but is adjusted based on current evidence. The MIND diet's near-elimination of cheese and blanket red meat restriction reflects outdated saturated fat dogma. The RCT evidence for MIND diet specifically improving cognition is weaker than observational studies suggest — exercise has stronger independent evidence for cognitive protection.

**Vitamin K2:** Aged hard cheeses are among the richest dietary sources of K2 (MK-4/MK-7), which activates matrix Gla protein and may inhibit arterial calcification — relevant given CAC score of 24.8 with LAD lesion.
